The problem

Detector type only works if it matches the smoke particle size

SAME measured smoke with mean particle diameters of 100 to 200 nm. Ionization detectors are most sensitive to small particles. Photoelectric and light scattering detectors, the type flown on the ISS today, are most sensitive to larger particles. The chart below plots both: particle diameter along the bottom, detector response up the side, with the SAME measurements shaded on the same chart.

Transport

Without buoyancy, smoke reaches a detector only as fast as the airflow carries it

In microgravity there is no buoyant rise, so smoke moves only with the cabin ventilation airflow. SAME simulated that transit by aging smoke for 10 to 1800 seconds in a chamber before measuring it, and in Saffire-V a particulate spike registered 57 seconds after airflow changed from 0 to 20 cm/s.

Understanding Smoke Detection

Would the ISS detector catch this material's smoke?

Choose a material. See the size of its smoke particles, which detectors can see them, and whether the ISS detector caught that smoke in testing.

Electronics bays, where Teflon and Kapton insulation dominate, are exactly where the current detector is weakest. The two materials with a detection gap are the two materials wrapped around spacecraft electronics.

Smoke particle diameter vs. detector response: the SAME measurements

This chart plots two things. Along the bottom: smoke particle diameter in nanometres on a log scale, with the size ranges SAME actually measured: a mean diameter of 100 to 200 nm, a substantial portion of particles under 500 nm, and Teflon smoke primarily under 1,000 nm. Up the side: which detector type responds to which sizes. Ionization detectors respond most to small particles; photoelectric and light scattering detectors, the type flown on the ISS today, respond most to larger particles. Each verdict above comes from where a material's measured smoke size falls against those two responses.

Why placement matters as much as the sensor

In microgravity, smoke does not rise. It travels with the ventilation airflow, and SAME aged smoke for 10 to 1800 seconds to simulate that trip. A well matched detector placed off the airflow path still alarms late.

What better equipment looks like

NASA and NIST concluded that no single detector is sensitive to all smoke particle sizes. The design direction the data points to is complementary sensing: a detector matched to submicron smoke alongside the photoelectric type, especially in electronics bays.

Materials to compare

Three kinds of label are used in the results. TESTED means a result from SAME or SAME-R testing. JUDGEMENT means a match judgement from particle size, not a per material alarm result. RECOMMENDATION means a design recommendation from this tool, not a test result.

Standing implication: wire insulation materials, Teflon and Kapton, are the blind spot risk. They wrap spacecraft electronics, and they are the two materials the ISS photoelectric detector missed in SAME testing.

Current Gaps

No partial gravity data yet

There is currently no partial gravity combustion data in PSI. LUCI (Lunar-g Combustion Investigation) and FM2 (Flammability of Materials on the Moon) are future investigations. Until they fly, microgravity and normal gravity results bound what happens at lunar gravity. A bound is not a measurement, and lunar verdicts should be read that way.

No per material alarm times published

No per material time to alarm in seconds exists in the public SAME summaries, so none is shown or invented anywhere in the tool. What does exist is shown instead: SAME's 10 to 1800 second smoke aging range, and Saffire-V's observed 57 seconds between an airflow change and a far field particulate spike.

Two materials have no published verdict

Cotton was tested in SAME-R and DBP was tested in SAME, but the public summaries publish no per material detector verdict for either. Understanding Smoke Detection marks both as not enough published data rather than guessing.

Who benefits, and how

Spacecraft designers

Which detection research applies to a lunar base running 30 percent oxygen or more, where everything burns faster, is answered in one ranked comparison instead of a 50 page guide.

Crew safety

Detection is the only fire protection layer that buys time. Prevention fails and suppression needs a located fire; the minutes detection adds are the minutes the crew acts in.

Researchers

The gaps become visible. No partial gravity data and no per material alarm times shows exactly what LUCI and FM2 need to measure when they fly.

NASA and Earth applications

The data was already paid for and flown; lining it up makes it usable by people who will never read the original reports. The same smoke versus dust discrimination problem runs industrial and mining alarms, where NASA detector research already led to detectors that cut nuisance alarms.
